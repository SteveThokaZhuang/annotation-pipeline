Review these three distinctions before writing the decisions. Timing and words are evidence; the audio vote is advisory.

1. An answer can interrupt a question. At the answer onset inspect recipient_clause_at_onset. If the questioner is still audibly delivering or repeating their request and the respondent takes over with a substantive answer, count the reverse interruption even though it answers that question. If the question has already yielded or the entry only crosses its final word without displacing any speech, DROP as an ordinary answer. A terminal_question_answer is a short polarity response lasting at most 0.6 seconds and beginning within 0.5 seconds of an intact question's end. Treat it as ordinary turn entry.
2. Re-evaluate each new clause after the interrupted person comes back in. Finishing a syntactically open fragment or completing a shared phrase does not by itself establish a separate bid. After a pause, if the same person starts a new assertion while the other party is still speaking and it competes with that ongoing contribution, KEEP a new event there. Related subject matter, and a prior contribution by that speaker, do not justify a MERGE. Conversely a tight repetition or elaboration within that new bid remains a continuation.
3. Read the clause the candidate cuts into, including connectors like "but" with no completion. A listener can echo the previous sentence as a way to take the floor, then hold it through a whole contribution while the original speaker stops. That is different from a quick receipt when the original speaker keeps their floor. Independently keep an opposite-direction acknowledgment while the new contribution is ongoing if it leaves that floor alone. For a short reply, inspect what the speaker actually responds to and whether the other speaker continues, instead of assigning by vocabulary alone.
